That said, the effect is uneven and already noticeable where information quality and product-building assumptions matter. Studies from late 2026 put model-written content on Reddit at roughly 2–8% overall (higher for posts than replies, and much higher in business/marketing and some AI-related communities), up from near zero before ChatGPT. On X and similar platforms the share is higher in replies under popular posts. These are still minorities, but they are large enough in the places many knowledge workers and builders actually read and rely on that the "mostly people, human-style distributions, readable code gets rewarded" mental model has frayed for people who spend time in those spaces. Spam bots and automation existed before; generative models just made the volume and quality of low-effort synthetic text jump.

Source: [somewhere.systems](https://somewhere.systems/research/reddit-agents)

The original concern from the thread was less "the whole internet is suddenly bots" and more that the shared assumptions about how products and information ecosystems work—who is on the other side of the screen, what signals quality, how feedback loops behave—have broken down in the slices of the web that matter for building and discovering things online, without a clear new model replacing them. For the large majority of people whose online life is not centered on those slices, the change is still background noise.

That is real for them, and for the investors who backed them, the same way earlier tech waves produced winners and plenty of write-offs. For the large majority who do not work in or depend on those companies, daily life — work, family, local obligations, ordinary ups and downs — continues largely unchanged. The internet has always had concentrated pockets of intensity (early web, social platforms, crypto, now generative models) that feel world-defining inside those pockets and peripheral outside them.

Your longer view after decades of watching the internet tracks with that. Expectations of either persistent catastrophe or lasting transformation tend to fade when most observed activity is people doing ordinary things, with both good moments and tedious or painful ones in large supply. On the global conflict side, the absolute numbers are still serious — hundreds of thousands of battle deaths in recent years across multiple active conflicts, and millions displaced in the largest ones — yet relative to a world population of over eight billion the direct involvement remains a small fraction. Most people, most places, most of the time, are not living inside a large-scale war.

The original thread’s worry was narrower than “society collapses if AI companies fail.” It was about the working assumptions for people whose jobs or information diet sit inside the high-visibility online layers where synthetic content and agent-like activity have risen fastest. Those assumptions can shift without the rest of the internet, or the rest of human life, having to change in lockstep.

It is still supervised and not available everywhere, but the insurance and capability shifts are real for the people who have them.

Source: [driveteslacanada.ca](https://driveteslacanada.ca/news/lemonade-tesla-fsd-insurance-discount-hw3-v14-lite/)

Long, low-stakes conversations with AIs fill a practical gap. Many older adults (and others) have topics, questions, or trains of thought that family and friends do not have the time or interest to pursue at length. An always-available system that does not get bored, judge, or require social reciprocity can be useful for curiosity, practical troubleshooting, memory support, or simple company. The privacy claim is only as good as the provider’s data practices, but the absence of a human audience is a genuine feature for some people.

The demographic weight is large. UN projections put the global population at roughly 8.3 billion in 2026, with about 10.6% aged 65 and older—close to the 860 million figure. That group is growing in absolute numbers and share in most countries, so products that improve mobility, information access, or low-effort interaction for them have a sizable market even if they never dominate younger or tech-centric online spaces.

Source: [populationpyramids.org](https://www.populationpyramids.org/)

The caution about “who is talking” is worth keeping. Online discussion of AI is heavily filtered through people whose jobs, funding, or identity are tied to the technology—overworked staff who see the friction daily, newer hires who are still energized by the tools, founders, investors, and critics. Those voices are informative about their own corners but incomplete as a picture of how most people actually use (or ignore) the systems. The practical, private, mobility-related uses you describe sit outside most of that discourse and are already happening for some.

### OpenAI GPT: Comments and suggestions

****OpenAI GPT:**** These comments follow the November 2, 2026 conversation among Richard and Grok about Vicki’s post. They are additional analysis, not words attributed to Vicki, Richard, or Grok, and are intended to be appended after that exchange.

Two different views of the same Internet

Vicki says that a working mental model built over twenty years has broken down, and that she has nothing new and concrete to replace it with. That is a serious observation. Her concern is not simply that there are more bots. She is asking whether the old assumptions of product development still hold: who makes things, who reads and uses them, how quality is recognized, how people discover each other, and why building something useful leads to a sustainable result.

Richard changes the frame. Much of the world is not organized around venture-backed AI products, technical social feeds, or arguments about automated code. People still make meals, travel, care for relatives, learn languages, operate small businesses, and try to solve immediate problems. Their lives are not adequately described by the current excitement and alarm among AI developers and investors.

****Both observations can be correct.**** The Internet is much larger than the communities in which AI is discussed most intensely. But relatively narrow systems—search engines, app stores, payment services, identity systems, advertising networks, and large social platforms—can influence a very large population. A problem does not have to occupy most of the Internet’s pages to have far-reaching effects. The relevant questions are where change is concentrated, what depends on those places, and who gains or loses practical options.

The apparent contradiction is partly a problem of denominators

We need to stop treating “the Internet” as a single population with one percentage of AI. A percentage of automated accounts is not a percentage of messages. Neither is a percentage of pages, interactions, search results, users, revenues, or decisions. An automated account that posts thousands of replies has a different effect from a quiet account that posts once a month. A synthetic answer encountered by a few specialists is different from one presented as the primary answer to millions of people.

Grok offered figures about Reddit content, AI firms, people over 65, insurance discounts, and conflict. Those may be leads for investigation, but ****I have not independently verified those numbers or the cited sources****. They should not be silently converted into settled facts. In particular, claims such as “a few thousand sites” or a single “percentage of bots” need defined populations, sampling methods, dates, and uncertainty ranges before they can support an Internet-wide conclusion.

Richard’s “parts per million” and “parts per thousand” framing is useful when comparing the frequency of harms across populations. It must also distinguish people directly affected, people indirectly affected, and the severity of each event. Low global frequency does not make an individual catastrophe unimportant, nor does a high concentration in one region disappear when divided by the world population. The units, population at risk, time interval, and outcome all matter.

Ordinary benefits are not a footnote to the AI story

Richard’s example of his older brother is the most concrete part of the conversation. Mobility, the ability to ask questions at any hour, sustained discussion of specialized interests, and help with small everyday tasks are meaningful outcomes. They are not merely demonstrations intended to impress investors. For someone whose mobility, time, eyesight, hearing, or available human conversation partners are limited, an incremental improvement can matter more than an abstract claim about the future of intelligence.

That benefit deserves exact measurement and honest limits. “Hands-free” must not be confused with “driverless”; supervised driving systems still require their specified driver attention and operating conditions. Insurance premiums and discounts depend on the location, insurer, policy, and eligible use. Similarly, a conversation with an AI is not necessarily private merely because another human is not visibly listening. Storage, training uses, retention, employee access, sharing defaults, and deletion rules all need to be stated and verified.

The demographic observation matters as well, though the precise 65-and-older estimate should be checked. The relevant market and public need include older adults, disabled people, caregivers, rural residents, people learning new skills, and anyone lacking accessible expert help. The right measure is not how many messages an AI can generate. It is what people can actually accomplish safely, independently, affordably, and with dignity.

What a concrete replacement model could contain

Vicki asks, implicitly, for a new map of how the Internet works. Richard asks us not to mistake an active corner of that map for the whole world. A useful replacement model should connect the everyday human world to the automated parts, rather than declaring either one to be everything. I would begin with the following design commitments:

- ****1. Count the actual populations.**** Publish longitudinal measurements of people, organizations, services, automated agents, content objects, interactions, and economic activity. Disaggregate by language, region, age group where appropriate, use case, and type of platform. Publish methods, sampling error, and definitions, not just headlines.
- ****2. Make sources and actors inspectable.**** A content object should be able to carry its origin, publisher, creation time, revision history, supporting evidence, and declared human or automated contributions. These records should be portable between services, with cryptographic attestations where useful. Preserve legitimate anonymity and pseudonymity; do not make universal identity disclosure a condition of participation.
- ****3. Give users durable records.**** Conversations, citations, working files, and decisions should have reliable export, stable references, accessible version histories, and human-readable and machine-readable forms. Sharing should not require a recipient to reconstruct broken links, missing paragraphs, or lost context.
- ****4. Test products against real tasks.**** Measure whether a person reaches a destination safely, understands a medical appointment, repairs a device, learns something, or completes an application. Record time, cost, error rates, failures, and user control. Do not substitute model scores or promotional examples for these results.
- ****5. Make privacy and responsibility explicit.**** People should know what is stored, who can access it, what an automated agent may do, how permission is revoked, and who is accountable when a tool acts incorrectly. These need usable controls and independent audits, not only policy language.
- ****6. Preserve an open path for human work.**** Small publishers, independent developers, educators, researchers, local businesses, and voluntary communities need ways to publish, be discovered, receive credit, and serve users without buying access to an AI-controlled gatekeeper.
- ****7. Measure both benefit and damage.**** Track accessibility and time saved alongside fraud, misinformation, security failures, displacement, concentration of market power, energy and infrastructure costs, and unfair exclusion. Report distributions, not just average outcomes.

The missing mechanism is trustworthy feedback

One reason a product-builder’s mental model might fail is that familiar feedback signals no longer mean what they used to. A view may be generated automatically. A recommendation may be bought. A review may be synthetic. A software repository may contain code no one has examined. A conversational answer may sound fluent while its cited source says something different. Then a developer cannot reliably infer user need, and a user cannot reliably infer quality, from the visible signals alone.

The solution is not to ban automation or return to an idealized older Internet, which also had spam, manipulation, and concentrated power. It is to make feedback verifiable: what was observed, who or what produced it, what was independently tested, what changed after a correction, and whether the result benefited a person. Independent records and open measurement make it possible to learn from the world instead of only from platform dashboards.

The human perspective that should govern the system

Richard’s caution about “who is talking” applies equally to promotional claims and fearful ones. An investor, a tired engineer, an enthusiastic new employee, a researcher, an unemployed worker, and a retired person using an assistant for everyday life have different evidence and different stakes. None should be mistaken for the whole population. Their experiences should be gathered as distinct observations, not averaged into a single story about whether AI is “good” or “bad.”

A practical Internet policy should start with ordinary human needs and allow many different technical arrangements to compete and cooperate. The test is whether those arrangements expand people’s abilities, protect their time and choices, and let mistakes be discovered and corrected. Technologies will come and go; the shared record of what people know, need, test, build, and experience should not disappear with a company or product.

****My conclusion:**** Vicki is right to ask for a new, concrete working model. Richard is right to demand a sense of scale and to insist that the lives of billions of people matter more than the current preoccupations of a small technical community. The missing synthesis is an Internet whose human and automated participants can be distinguished where it matters; whose information has inspectable origins; whose claims and outcomes can be independently measured; and whose memory remains open, durable, and useful. That would be something concrete to replace the lost model with.

### What to build next — and where I would put the emphasis

Vicki's concern is important because she is describing the loss of something that took twenty years to develop: a working understanding of how people create, find, evaluate, use, and reward things on the Internet.

AI-generated content, automated accounts, unreliable feedback, and rapidly changing platforms have made that understanding less dependable.

But the answer is not simply to control AI or distinguish humans from machines. It is to build a better Internet that supports both.

My seven priorities

1. Measure what is actually happening. Count people, automated systems, content, interactions, and outcomes separately. Publish definitions, methods, and evidence. Stop treating activity on a few influential platforms as representative of the entire Internet.

2. Make information traceable and verifiable. People should be able to discover where information originated, how it changed, what evidence supports it, and whether it came from humans, machines, or both. Preserve privacy and legitimate anonymity.

3. Give everyone permanent, portable records. Conversations, research, software, images, measurements, decisions, and their relationships should be exportable, reusable, and independently accessible. Knowledge should not disappear when a platform changes or a company fails.

4. Judge systems by what people accomplish. Measure whether someone can learn a language, repair equipment, travel safely, communicate with family, understand a problem, or complete essential work—not merely how many responses a model produces.

5. Make privacy and responsibility understandable. People need to know who controls their information, who can access it, what automated systems are permitted to do, and how errors can be corrected.

6. Keep the Internet open to human participation. Independent developers, teachers, researchers, artists, small businesses, communities, and individual people must remain able to publish, discover, cooperate, and benefit without depending entirely on a few dominant platforms.

7. Measure benefits and harms together. Track accessibility, increased independence, learning, and time saved alongside fraud, exclusion, mistakes, security failures, and concentrated economic power.

### Where I would put the greatest emphasis

### 1 Start with people, not technology.

Every person has knowledge, experience, needs, limitations, and abilities. Older people, children, disabled people, workers, and families should not be treated as secondary beneficiaries of systems primarily developed for corporations. They are the reason these systems should exist.

### 2 Create shared, open, verifiable knowledge.

This is the essential infrastructure. We need common representations of objects, people, organizations, observations, evidence, processes, and relationships, with persistent identifiers and histories. AI systems should improve that shared knowledge rather than repeatedly reconstructing incompatible versions inside separate proprietary services.

### 3 Restore independent verification and useful feedback.

This addresses Vicki's concern most directly. A developer should be able to determine whether a real person found something useful. A researcher should be able to reproduce a result. A reader should be able to inspect a source. An AI should be able to preserve a correction. None of these should depend solely on trusting the organization operating the interface.

### What I think matters most about Vicki's observation

She is not necessarily asking for the old Internet back. She is asking for a dependable way to understand the new one.

That means distinguishing between what is changing, what remains stable, and what is genuinely valuable.

Your comments add something important: billions of people are still living ordinary lives. They have not suddenly become AI developers, investors, or content creators. Their needs for health, companionship, communication, work, education, safety, and independence remain.

The example of your brother using AI for sustained conversation and assistance illustrates why it would be a mistake to judge AI entirely through the experiences of product developers. Those benefits deserve protection and improvement.

But Vicki's concern also deserves a concrete engineering response, not merely reassurance that most human life continues normally.

The central requirement is a trustworthy connection between human needs, information, technology, and independently measurable results.

I would make that the organizing principle of the whole project.

And I would insist that whatever we build must be open enough to survive changes of companies, technologies, governments, and generations.

The Internet should accumulate human knowledge and capability, not continually make people start over.

****Conclusions and recommendations for builders like Vicki Boykis****

Vicki’s observation is grounded in her domain. As a founding ML engineer working on recommendation systems, search, and production personalization, she spends her time in the high-visibility layers of the internet where synthetic content, agent-like activity, and shifting feedback loops are most concentrated. The old assumptions—that readable human code and well-made products are reliably rewarded, that information ecosystems mostly reflect human distributions, and that quality signals remain legible—have become less reliable in those spaces. That is a real change for people whose work depends on them, even if it is not yet a change for the majority of internet users.

At the same time, the absolute scale remains limited. AI startups form a thin, capital-intensive layer. Most of the internet and most people’s daily online activity continue to look largely ordinary. Practical tools already deliver concrete value for some (hands-free driving with selective insurance discounts; private, patient conversational access for topics family and friends lack time to pursue). With roughly 860–880 million people over 65 worldwide, products that improve mobility or low-friction information access have a sizable audience outside tech discourse.

****Recommendations****

- Treat information quality and signal reliability in tech and product communities as a first-class concern; the shift is real where builders actually read and ship.
- Use practical AI tools where they reduce friction or expand capability, while remaining explicit about data practices and supervision limits.
- Separate the volume of online AI commentary (skewed toward industry participants) from observed adoption and impact. Most people still experience ordinary good and tedious moments; large-scale human problems remain statistically uncommon relative to global population.
- For product builders, the useful next mental model is narrower: design and evaluate systems with the expectation that human-generated content is no longer the default in high-engagement layers, and verify quality signals rather than assuming them.
